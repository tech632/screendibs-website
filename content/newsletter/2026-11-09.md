## Streamers are buying proven books, some over two decades old!

> A book's screen life can begin even after its publication peak

📚 Netflix just ordered a series from a novel readers have loved for two decades.

Netflix has given Prep, Curtis Sittenfeld's debut novel, a straight-to-series order, per Deadline and TheWrap. Nicole Taylor, who created One Day, is writing and running the show, and Sittenfeld called it a 22-year wait.

### Why it matters:

- ⏳ **Age is no barrier:** A book's screen life can begin long after its publication peak.
- 🎬 **Writer first:** Taylor says she wanted to adapt Prep for twenty years, and her attachment helped carry it to an order.
- 🌏 **Indies move too:** Dollhouse Pictures secured screen rights to Bri Lee's Australian bestseller The Work, per Variety Australia.

**Sd* Pro Tip:** Backlist titles with a loyal readership and a committed writer attached remain some of the safest bets a buyer can make.

## An unclear contract just opened a door for new ‘Lord of the Flies’ series

> Older deals often never contemplated for TV, leaves rights with the family.

🔍 One unclear line in an old contract made a new Lord of the Flies possible.

Eleven Film's Joel Wilson told the Thessaloniki Film Festival that Warner Bros. had held rights to William Golding's novel for years, per Deadline. The original contract was unclear on whether it covered television or only film.

### Why it matters:

- 📜 **Read the paper:** Older deals often never contemplated TV, and that silence can leave rights with the author's family.
- 🤝 **Patience pays:** Wilson kept calling the estate and the stage-rights agent for years before the door opened.
- ✍️ **Attach talent:** The BBC greenlit the series with Jack Thorne attached before a script existed.

Many classic titles were licensed under contracts written before streaming, or even before television mattered.

## Asian readers signal how free chapters translate into paid sales

> In Japan, almost 70% of free readers are buying source IP, signaling an audience ready for adaptations.

📱 In Japan, the cliffhanger is what turns free readers into buyers.

An Oricon survey of 8,668 digital comics readers found 59.3% mainly read free content, per Publishing Perspectives. Of free readers who have bought, 68.9% paid to find out what happens next.

### What else the week showed:

- 🌐 **Free in English:** Kadokawa's World Manga label now offers free English translations of first and latest chapters of selected series.
- 🇰🇷 **Korea reads:** Bookstore sales rose 8.5% in the first nine months of 2026, per The Korea Times.
- 👥 **Younger buyers:** Readers in their twenties and thirties made up 26% of Korean bookstore sales.

**Sd* Pro Tip:** Free sampling can build an audience, and show which series travel, before rights are sold abroad.
